\begin{center}
{\sffamily\bfseries\small ABSTRACT}
\end{center}
\vspace{-0.5em}
\noindent{\small
Autonomous AI agents plan multi-step workflows, invoke external APIs, modify
application state, and delegate sub-tasks with minimal real-time human
supervision---exposing a systemic vulnerability we call the
\emph{Verifiability Gap}: the structural impossibility of confirming that an
agent operated within its authorized policy boundaries and that its controls
remained active and unmanipulated. Legacy mechanisms (system prompts, static
access control, embedded guardrails, post-hoc log auditing) fail against
non-deterministic, path-dependent execution at machine speed. We introduce
\poc{} (PoC), an open standard for runtime verifiability comprising: (i) six
\emph{domains of verification} enumerating 111 auditable requirements;
(ii) four \emph{Verifiability Tiers} grading evidence by who must be trusted
to believe it, with a \emph{binary threshold} separating authenticated
documentation from mechanism-generated evidence; (iii) a reference
architecture coupling lifecycle interception hooks with hardware-isolated
policy evaluation under IETF RATS (RFC~9334), emitting Entity Attestation
Tokens over a signed Merkle execution chain; and (iv) a reproducible coverage
mapping showing that eight leading frameworks (NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC~42001,
OWASP AISVS, EU AI Act, SOC~2, CSA AARM, NIST SP~800-207, MITRE ATLAS)
address 27--63\% of PoC requirements---almost entirely as partial matches
that require the control but not operator-independent evidence of it. We
state a formal policy model and an adversary model with four security
properties, proving execution fidelity under capability-bound dispatch and
showing that two properties do \emph{not} follow from attestation alone.
We report an open-source reference implementation and its measurement: the
software pipeline costs \SI{191}{\micro\second} per intercepted step
(p99 \SI{237}{\micro\second}), 1.3\% of a \SI{15}{\milli\second} budget;
path-aware evaluation is flat at \SI{0.21}{\micro\second} across a
500$\times$ increase in path length, versus linear growth for naive
re-evaluation; and eight attacks---including snapshot substitution,
truncation, and split-view---succeed without the derived requirements and are
refused or detected with them. The utility measurement is the consequential
one: path-aware authorization falsely rejects 42.2\% of benign workflows
without an explicit declassification point and 0\% with one, reproducing
the label-creep result from information-flow control.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The rapid evolution from passive natural-language generation to autonomous
agentic execution represents a foundational shift in computational
infrastructure~\cite{paloalto2026,airia2026}. Autonomous agents now plan
multi-step operational workflows, query complex databases, invoke external
APIs, modify application state, and delegate sub-tasks to secondary agents
with minimal real-time human supervision. While this autonomy unlocks
substantial productivity, it exposes a systemic architectural vulnerability:
the \emph{Verifiability Gap}---the absence of independent evidence of what an
AI system did~\cite{pocspec}.

The Verifiability Gap describes the structural impossibility of
confirming---either during execution or retrospectively---that an autonomous
agent operated within its authorized policy boundaries, that mandatory
security controls remained active and uncompromised, and that the agent's
state transitions were free from malicious manipulation or reasoning failure.
Because agents execute complex, non-deterministic actions at machine speed,
human oversight and traditional log-auditing workflows cannot maintain
effective operational control~\cite{catalini2026}. Existing governance
implementations rely almost exclusively on operator self-attestation, static
permissions, or unanchored application logs, none of which provide open,
cryptographically verifiable proof of policy
compliance.

\paragraph{Contributions and scope.} This paper contributes:
(1)~the \poc{} standard---six domains of verification, four evidence
properties, four Verifiability Tiers with a binary threshold, four
requirement levels aligned to the tiers, and three conformance stages---as a
catalogue of 116 auditable requirements (Section~\ref{sec:standard});
(2)~a reference architecture integrating lifecycle interception with
hardware-backed attestation under IETF RATS~\cite{rfc9334}, Entity
Attestation Tokens~\cite{rfc9711}, and a signed hash-chained execution
history in the tamper-evident-logging
lineage~\cite{merkle1987,crosby2009,rfc6962} (Section~\ref{sec:arch});
(3)~a formal policy model and an adversary model with four stated security
properties, the assumptions each depends on, and two attacks
(snapshot substitution, split-view) that the architecture does \emph{not}
resist without the additional requirements we derive here
(Sections~\ref{sec:formal}--\ref{sec:security});
(4)~a descriptive gap analysis mapping all 116 requirements against eight
regulatory and security frameworks, with its methodological limits stated
(Section~\ref{sec:gap}); and
(5)~an open-source reference implementation, an eight-scenario attack
harness that runs each attack with and without the requirement that addresses
it, and measurements of latency, path-length scaling, verification cost, and
the utility cost of path-aware authorization
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

\emph{Scope of the implementation.} A hardware TEE was not available; the
attesting environment is modelled in-process, so reported latencies exclude
enclave transition costs and are a lower bound on end-to-end deployment
latency. Everything else---signing, chaining, canonicalization, policy
evaluation, capability minting and checking, anchoring, gossip, and
verification---is real code exercised by real attacks. We state this boundary
explicitly rather than extrapolating an overhead we did not measure.

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Path dependency and non-deterministic execution}
Unlike legacy software executing deterministic decision trees, agents operate
within LLM reasoning loops that yield non-deterministic, path-dependent
trajectories. Given a single objective, an agent
dynamically determines its intermediate steps, selects tools, interprets
unstructured outputs, and modifies its path from real-time observations.
Compliance therefore cannot be validated prior to deployment, and evaluating
an individual tool call in isolation fails to mitigate path-dependent risk:
an agent reading confidential records early and issuing an outbound network
request later creates a cumulative exfiltration risk that single-step
inspection cannot detect. Empirical agent-security benchmarks establish both
the attack surface and the weakness of per-call defenses: indirect prompt
injection reliably hijacks tool-integrated agents through retrieved
content~\cite{greshake2023,injecagent}, sandboxed emulation surfaces harmful
trajectories that per-step review misses~\cite{toolemu}, and dynamic agent
environments show that defenses effective on isolated prompts degrade over
multi-step tool-calling sequences~\cite{agentdojo,agentharm}. The underlying
structure is not new: a sequence of individually permitted operations that
together violate a policy is the classical information-flow
problem~\cite{denning1976,goguen1982}, which we return to in
Section~\ref{sec:ifc}.

\subsection{Why legacy governance fails}
Current AI risk management relies on three primary control mechanisms, none
of which resolves the Verifiability Gap.

\emph{System prompts and in-context instructions} inhabit the same logical
stream as untrusted user inputs, retrieved documents, and tool outputs; this
co-mingling renders them vulnerable to direct and indirect prompt injection.
Prompting is a probabilistic steering mechanism, not a deterministic
boundary: if a model deviates from its system prompt, no execution barrier
prevents the action.

\emph{Role-based access control and static identity} restrict which
endpoints an agent may call but operate without contextual awareness. An
agent holding valid credentials for both internal database reads and
external messaging retains the technical capability to exfiltrate data;
static IAM cannot evaluate the context, sequence, or cumulative risk of
individually authorized permissions~\cite{sp800207}.

\emph{Application-embedded guardrails} run under the agent's own execution
authority. For agents with code-generation or system-command tools, internal
guardrails provide weak isolation---a compromised process can bypass its own
application-level checks---and they generate local, unauthenticated logs
that cannot be audited independently of the host
operator~\cite{netbankaudit2026}.

\subsection{The collapse of the post-hoc audit window}
The operational window for an autonomous exploit has collapsed toward zero:
indirect prompt injections embedded in public web pages or internal
repositories can hijack a reasoning loop instantly~\cite{airia2026}.
Retrospective SIEM auditing is insufficient because application logs lack
cryptographic immutability; an operator---or an adversary with
persistence---can modify, append, or delete entries post-execution.
Verification must therefore shift from reactive log auditing to inline,
machine-speed cryptographic attestation anchored to hardware roots of
trust~\cite{redhat2026,eqty2026}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Runtime governance for agents.} The Agent Control Specification
defines portable interception middleware, policy manifests, and semantic
conventions for agent runtimes~\cite{acs2026,acsms2026}, and CSA's AARM defines
approve/modify/defer/deny enforcement at the action
boundary~\cite{csaaarm}. These supply the \emph{enforcement} half of agentic
assurance; \poc{} supplies the complementary \emph{evidence} half---the
operator-independent proof that enforcement occurred.

\paragraph{Remote attestation and confidential computing.} The IETF RATS
architecture~\cite{rfc9334,ratsarch}, Entity Attestation
Tokens~\cite{rfc9711,tcg2024}, multi-verifier
topologies~\cite{multiverifier}, and hardware TEEs with verifiable-compute
toolchains~\cite{redhat2026,eqty2026} provide the mechanism families \poc{}
builds on. These prove properties of \emph{platforms and artifacts}; \poc{}
extends them into a conformance regime for \emph{agent behavior}.

\paragraph{Empirical agent-safety evaluation.} Benchmarks for
tool-integrated agents---AgentDojo~\cite{agentdojo},
InjecAgent~\cite{injecagent}, ToolEmu~\cite{toolemu}, and
AgentHarm~\cite{agentharm}---establish that defenses evaluated on isolated
prompts degrade over multi-step trajectories, and that harmful outcomes
frequently arise from sequences of individually plausible tool calls. \poc{}
absorbs this as a normative requirement (path-aware authorization) rather
than as a detection heuristic.

\paragraph{Identity substrate.} Decentralized identity work---W3C
DIDs~\cite{w3cdid} and the Verifiable Credentials data
model~\cite{w3cvc2}---together with supply-chain attestation
formats~\cite{slsa,c2pa} provides the identity and provenance substrate the
C1 and C5 domains build on.

\paragraph{Tamper-evident logging and transparency.} The evidence chain is a
direct descendant of Merkle authentication trees~\cite{merkle1987}, history
trees for tamper-evident logging~\cite{crosby2009}, and Certificate
Transparency~\cite{rfc6962,rfc9162}. That literature also supplies the
attacks we must resist: a log operator can equivocate (present divergent
views to different auditors) or truncate, which is why CT requires gossip and
consistency proofs~\cite{ctgossip,chase2016}, and why systems such as
CHAINIAC add collective signing~\cite{chainiac}. Section~\ref{sec:security}
applies these results to agent evidence; to our knowledge, prior agent
governance work does not.

\paragraph{Reference monitors and complete mediation.} The requirement that
\emph{every} security-relevant operation be mediated by a tamper-proof,
always-invoked, verifiable mechanism is the reference-monitor
concept~\cite{anderson1972} and the complete-mediation
principle~\cite{saltzer1975}. Section~\ref{sec:mediation} argues that agent
evidence architectures satisfy the ``verifiable'' clause via attestation but
must be designed explicitly for ``always-invoked,'' and that a system meeting
only the former produces an authenticated record that may not describe the
action that actually executed.

\paragraph{TEE limitations.} Enclave-based designs inherit a documented
attack surface---architectural disclosure~\cite{costan2016}, transient
execution and side-channel attacks~\cite{foreshadow}---and a vendor-rooted
trust anchor. Section~\ref{sec:security} states these as explicit assumptions
rather than treating hardware attestation as trust-free.

\paragraph{Standards and regulation.} NIST AI RMF~\cite{nistairmf} and its
generative-AI profile~\cite{nist6001}, ISO/IEC 42001~\cite{iso42001} and
23894~\cite{iso23894}, SOC~2~\cite{soc2tsc}, the EU AI Act~\cite{euaiact},
OWASP AISVS~\cite{owaspaisvs}, MITRE ATLAS~\cite{mitreatlas}, and zero-trust
architecture~\cite{sp800207} govern adjacent layers; Section~\ref{sec:eval}
quantifies exactly how much of \poc{} each already covers, following the
coverage-mapping methodology introduced by HAARF for healthcare agent
regulation~\cite{haarf2026}.

\section{The \poc{} Standard}
\label{sec:standard}

\poc{} is \emph{open verification} that the controls governing an agent
system are implemented and hold, graded by how independently that can be
verified. For each control it claims, across six domains, a conformant
implementation \textsc{must} produce evidence---generated by the enforcing
mechanism at execution time---that the control held; place that evidence on
the Verifiability Tiers; and disclose its residual trust assumptions. A
system has \poc{} when, and only when, its evidence reaches Tier~3 or
Tier~4~\cite{pocspec}. Requirements language follows RFC~2119~\cite{rfc2119}.

\subsection{Six domains of verification}
The standard enumerates 111 requirements across six domains, each stated as
a testable \emph{verify-that} obligation with auditor evidence guidance:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Provenance} (C1): which model ran; lineage, artifact, and
  supply-chain origin, bound by hash-linked custody chains and signed
  manifests~\cite{slsa,c2pa}.
\item \textbf{Privacy} (C2): what data was read and written, evidenced
  without re-leaking the data being protected~\cite{euaiact}.
\item \textbf{Portability} (C3): boundary crossings---organizational,
  jurisdictional, compute---with signed linking records so the evidence
  chain survives migrations.
\item \textbf{Authorization} (C4): authority granted, decisions within or
  against it, delegation validity; \emph{path-aware} evaluation so a
  composed sequence of individually authorized calls cannot exceed the
  authority of its steps (Section~\ref{sec:ifc}).
\item \textbf{Identity} (C5): which agent and which principal ran, bound by
  short-lived delegation tokens, DIDs, and verifiable
  credentials~\cite{w3cdid,w3cvc2}.
\item \textbf{Security} (C6): integrity of the execution environment,
  controls held, tools invoked, key lifecycle in hardware-backed custody.
\end{enumerate}

Four cross-cutting chapters govern evidence generation and properties
(binary, contemporaneous, tamper-evident, transparent), tier grading,
system-surface location on the seven MAESTRO layers~\cite{csamaestro}, and
conformance with standardized trust-assumption disclosure.

\subsection{Verifiability Tiers and the binary threshold}
\label{sec:tiers}

Evidence is graded by \emph{who must be trusted to believe it}
(Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}). Cryptography alone does not raise the tier;
removing the trusted party does. An operator-signed log is cryptographic and
still Tier~2. A vendor-rooted TEE attestation is Tier~2 unless composed with
independent anchoring (e.g., a public transparency log). The \emph{binary
threshold} falls between Tier~2 and Tier~3: below it, authenticated
documentation; above it, mechanism-generated evidence. The threshold makes
the category procurable---``does your AI have \poc{}?'' is a yes-or-no
question, the pattern used by every category-defining certification
(FIPS~140, Common Criteria, PCI~DSS, CSA STAR).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{figures/tier-ladder.pdf}
\caption{The four Verifiability Tiers. The binary threshold falls between
Tiers 2 and 3: below it, authenticated documentation (a party must still be
trusted); above it, mechanism-generated evidence. Requirement levels 1--4
align one-to-one with the tiers; meeting all Level 1--3 requirements in the
claimed domains is the minimum for a \poc{} claim.}
\label{fig:ladder}
\end{figure}

Conformance is graded on a separate axis by three named stages---%
Self-Declared, Third-Party Assessed, Continuously Monitored---each requiring
a standardized conformance statement with declared system boundary, in-scope
action classes, per-claim tiers, mechanisms, and trust-assumption
disclosure. The declared inventory \textsc{must} be reconciled against
automated discovery from observability streams so undeclared (``shadow'')
agents surface as findings.

\section{Reference Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

The reference architecture integrates runtime lifecycle interception with
hardware-backed attestation under the IETF RATS architecture
(RFC~9334)~\cite{rfc9334,ratsarch}. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the
end-to-end pipeline and its mapping onto RATS roles: every consequential
action is intercepted, reduced to a canonical state snapshot, evaluated
inside a hardware enclave that the operator cannot reach into, and released
only after signed evidence exists; Figure~\ref{fig:flow} shows the same
boundary from the evidence-generation perspective.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/reference-architecture.pdf}
\caption{The \poc{} reference architecture and its IETF RATS role mapping.
The agent host (Attester) routes every lifecycle event through an ACS
interception point; the hardware TEE enclave (Attesting Environment)
verifies the policy bundle, evaluates the snapshot, extends the signed
Merkle chain, and emits the Entity Attestation Token (Evidence), which a
Verifier checks against published reference values before a Relying Party
executes the request.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/evidence-flow.pdf}
\caption{Evidence generation at the action boundary. The out-of-band Action
Interception Gateway mediates every tool call, writes evidence before the
action is released, and emits records any verifier can check without
privileged access; out-of-scope actions are rejected and evidenced.}
\label{fig:flow}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Lifecycle interception hooks}
\poc{} mandates standardized middleware hooks across the agent execution
loop, leveraging the open Agent Control Specification
(ACS)~\cite{acs2026,acsms2026}: \emph{task initialization}, \emph{context
assembly} (RAG/memory injection), \emph{plan generation}, \emph{pre-call
tool invocation}, \emph{post-call tool result}, \emph{memory write},
\emph{sub-agent delegation} (with constraint inheritance), and \emph{task
completion}. At each point the host constructs a canonical state
snapshot---agent identity, execution history, proposed action, environmental
context---submitted to an isolated policy engine that returns a binding
decision: \textsc{allow}, \textsc{deny}, \textsc{modify} (inline
sanitization), or \textsc{escalate} (human-in-the-loop authorization). The
gateway does not forward an action until the corresponding evidence record
is durably written, and in-scope actions fail closed when evidence cannot be
generated at the claimed tier.

\subsection{RATS role mapping}
The runtime maps onto RATS roles~\cite{rfc9334}: the \emph{Attester} is the
platform housing the agent runtime, hooks, and policy engine; the
\emph{Target Environment} is the operational layer containing the runtime,
model context, and proposed action; the \emph{Attesting Environment} is a
cryptographically isolated TEE (Intel TDX, AMD SEV-SNP, NVIDIA Confidential
Computing)~\cite{redhat2026,eqty2026} that measures the policy-engine
binaries, verifies the policy-bundle signature, evaluates the snapshot, and
signs the proof; \emph{Evidence} is an Entity Attestation Token (CWT/JWT)
carrying measurements, claims, step index, verdict, and platform
signatures~\cite{rfc9711,tcg2024}; a \emph{Verifier} compares Evidence
against reference values to produce an Attestation Result; and
\emph{Relying Parties} (API gateways, downstream services) require valid
results before executing tool requests. Sub-agent delegation chains are
governed by composite multi-verifier topologies~\cite{multiverifier}.

\subsection{Complete mediation: binding the snapshot to the effect}
\label{sec:mediation}

An enclave that evaluates a snapshot attests a precise statement: \emph{given
this snapshot, the authorized policy produced this verdict}. It does not, by
itself, attest that the snapshot faithfully described the action the host
subsequently performed. If the host constructs the snapshot and separately
holds the tool credentials and network path, a compromised host can submit
snapshot $\sigma$ describing a benign action, obtain an \textsc{allow} token,
and dispatch a different action $a^{\dagger}$---producing evidence that is
cryptographically impeccable and materially false. We call this
\emph{snapshot substitution}; it is the agent-governance instance of the
classical reference-monitor requirement that mediation be
always-invoked~\cite{anderson1972,saltzer1975}, and it is not closed by
attestation alone.

\poc{} therefore requires that the \emph{effect channel} be mediated within
the same trust boundary as policy evaluation. Concretely, at least one of the
following must hold for every in-scope action class, and which one must be
stated in the conformance claim:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Enclave-terminated egress.} The credentials and transport used
  to perform the effect are held inside the Attesting Environment, which
  emits the request itself after evaluation; the host cannot reach the tool
  endpoint independently.
\item \textbf{Capability-bound dispatch.} The enclave releases a
  single-use, action-bound capability (a token cryptographically committing
  to $H(\sigma_t^{\mathrm{snap}})$ and the target resource) that the relying
  party verifies before executing, so an unbound request is rejected at the
  far end.
\item \textbf{Attested-path mediation.} Egress is confined to a network path
  whose enforcement point is itself attested and configured to admit only
  requests carrying a valid, matching Evidence token.
\end{enumerate}

Option~2 requires cooperation from relying parties and is the only one that
survives a fully compromised host without enclave-terminated I/O; options~1
and~3 shrink the trusted computing base rather than eliminating host
involvement. A deployment that adopts none of these may still claim Tiers~1--2,
but \textsc{must not} claim that its evidence describes executed actions.

\subsection{Merkle-chain execution history}
Each intercepted step $t$ generates a leaf
\begin{equation}
L_t = H\!\left(L_{t-1} \,\|\, H(\sigma_t^{\mathrm{snap}}) \,\|\, d_t\right),
\qquad L_0 = H(\mathrm{seed}_{\mathrm{init}}),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_t^{\mathrm{snap}}$ is the canonical snapshot, $d_t$ the
verdict, and $H$ a collision-resistant hash. The Attesting Environment signs
each leaf with an enclave-bound key; per-source monotonic sequence indices
make \emph{omission} detectable within a chain a verifier observes
contiguously. Following the tamper-evident-logging
literature~\cite{merkle1987,crosby2009}, the accumulated root is periodically
anchored to a public transparency log or distributed trust root
(asynchronously, off the hot path). As Section~\ref{sec:security} shows,
hash chaining and anchoring alone do not resist equivocation or truncation:
the standard additionally requires bounded anchoring intervals and
cross-verifier consistency checking, in the manner of Certificate
Transparency gossip~\cite{rfc6962,ctgossip,chase2016}.

\subsection{Canonical evidence schema}
\label{sec:schema}

attestation submodule. Listing~\ref{lst:eat} shows a representative token
(JWT rendering; the CBOR/CWT encoding is byte-equivalent in claim
structure), produced at the pre-call tool-invocation hook for a payment
API request.

{

First, \emph{payloads never leave the boundary}: the token carries
\texttt{canonical\_snapshot\_hash}, a commitment to the full evaluated

evidence store ever holding raw data (the C2 privacy requirements).
Second, \emph{history is load-bearing}: \texttt{step\_index} and
\texttt{merkle\_root} bind this token to every token before it, which is
what makes silent omission of an inconvenient step as detectable as
alteration. Third, \emph{the policy is pinned}:
\texttt{policy\_bundle\_hash} identifies the exact rule set that produced
the verdict, so ``which policy was in force?'' is a claim to check, not a
change-log to reconstruct.

\texttt{mr\_config} to the published golden measurement of the policy
engine; (3)~check \texttt{policy\_bundle\_hash} against the signed bundle
registry; (4)~confirm \texttt{nonce} freshness; (5)~confirm
\texttt{step\_index} continuity and \texttt{merkle\_root} consistency
with previously seen tokens for this \texttt{agent\_id}; and only then
(6)~accept \texttt{verdict} as evidence that the control held for this
action. Every step uses published materials---no privileged access to the
operator's infrastructure is required, which is precisely what places this
evidence above the binary threshold.

\section{Formal Model}
\label{sec:formal}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be the space of agent actions, $\mathcal{I}$ the space of
cryptographically verifiable agent identities, and $\mathcal{S}$ the space
of host state snapshots. The executed steps up to time $t$ form the partial
path $p_t = \langle a_0, a_1, \dots, a_{t-1} \rangle \in \mathcal{A}^{*}$. A
compliance policy is a deterministic evaluation function
\begin{equation}
\Pi : \mathcal{A} \times \mathcal{I} \times \mathcal{S} \times
\mathcal{A}^{*} \longrightarrow \mathcal{D},
\qquad
\mathcal{D} = \{\textsc{allow},\ \textsc{deny},\
               \textsc{modify}(a'),\ \textsc{escalate}(h)\},
\end{equation}
where $a' \in \mathcal{A}$ is a sanitized payload and $h$ a human-oversight
routing profile. \poc{} requires $\Pi$ to execute inside an isolated
enclave, producing per-step proof
\begin{equation}
\pi_t = \mathrm{Sign}_{k_{\mathrm{AE}}}\!\left(
  H(\sigma_t^{\mathrm{snap}}) \,\|\, d_t \,\|\, t \,\|\, L_t \right),
\end{equation}
with $k_{\mathrm{AE}}$ accessible exclusively to the Attesting Environment.
The path argument makes the policy \emph{path-aware}: $\Pi$ evaluates the
proposed action against context carried from upstream invocations, so
composition attacks that are invisible to per-action evaluation are within
the decision boundary. A deliberate \emph{determinism
boundary} limits claims: \poc{} verifies deterministic facts of execution
(which model ran, on what input, under which authority, producing which
effect); it does not represent output correctness, model reasoning,
fairness, or future behavior as verified. Verification is performed, not
validation~\cite{iso42001}.

\subsection{Relation to information-flow control}
\label{sec:ifc}

Path-aware authorization is deliberately close to, and strictly weaker than,
information-flow control (IFC). The motivating hazard---an agent reads
sensitive data at step $i$ and emits at step $j$, with each step individually
permitted---is the canonical IFC scenario, formalized as noninterference by
Goguen and Meseguer~\cite{goguen1982} over Denning's lattice
model~\cite{denning1976,denning1977} and realized in language- and
OS-level systems~\cite{myers1997,myers1999,sabelfeld2003,histar,flume}. We
inherit both the framing and the known difficulties, and we state the
differences precisely rather than reinventing the field:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Granularity.} Classical IFC tracks labels at variable, object,
  or process granularity inside a trusted runtime. \poc{} tracks at the
  granularity of \emph{intercepted actions} at a boundary, because the agent
  runtime (an LLM loop with opaque internal state) is precisely what we
  decline to trust. We therefore obtain a coarser but externally checkable
  approximation.
\item \textbf{Objective.} IFC systems \emph{enforce} a flow property, often
  with a soundness theorem relative to a semantics. \poc{} enforces a policy
  \emph{and produces evidence} that the enforcement occurred, verifiable by a
  third party who did not observe execution. Evidence, not enforcement, is
  the contribution; the enforcement is an IFC-style monitor.
\item \textbf{Guarantee.} We claim no noninterference result. An agent whose
  permitted actions are themselves harmful, or which leaks through channels
  outside the intercepted set (timing, content of permitted egress), is
  outside the claim---consistent with the determinism boundary.
\item \textbf{Inherited problems.} Label creep and the declassification
  problem~\cite{sabelfeld2003} predict directly the utility cost we flag as
  unmeasured (Section~\ref{sec:eval}): a monitor that accumulates
  restrictions along a path will eventually refuse legitimate work, and the
  practical question is where declassification points belong. IFC's history
  suggests this, not cryptography, is the binding constraint on deployment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Path state and evaluation complexity}
\label{sec:complexity}

Evaluating $\Pi$ against the full path $p_t$ is not implementable as written:
$|p_t|$ grows without bound, and a per-step cost linear in $t$ violates the
latency target of Section~\ref{sec:eval}. \poc{} therefore requires
policies to be expressible over a \emph{bounded path summary}: a state
$\phi_t$ with $|\phi_t| \le B$ for a fixed $B$, updated by a fold
\begin{equation}
\phi_t = \mathsf{upd}(\phi_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, d_{t-1}), \qquad
\phi_0 = \phi_{\mathrm{init}},
\end{equation}
so that $\Pi(a, \iota, s, p_t) = \tilde{\Pi}(a, \iota, s, \phi_t)$ for the
implemented $\tilde{\Pi}$. The summary is the IFC analogue of a label: it
carries the accumulated sensitivity, authority, and provenance facts the
policy needs (for example, ``a record classified $c$ has been read'',
``$n$ tool calls remain within budget''), and nothing else.

Two consequences follow. \emph{(i)~Cost.} Per-step evaluation is
$O(|\tilde{\Pi}| + B)$, independent of path length, and the snapshot
committed in evidence includes $H(\phi_t)$ so a verifier can confirm the
policy was evaluated against the correct accumulated state. \emph{(ii)~
Expressiveness limit.} Policies requiring unbounded history---``never
contact an endpoint not seen in the first $k$ steps'' for unbounded $k$, or
exact multiset equality over arbitrary-length paths---are outside the
implementable fragment and must be approximated. This is a real restriction,
and it is the price of a fixed per-step budget; we state it rather than
leaving path-awareness unbounded and unimplementable.

\section{Adversary Model and Security Analysis}
\label{sec:security}

\subsection{Adversary model}
We consider a probabilistic polynomial-time adversary $\mathcal{A}$ that may:
(i)~fully control the host operating system, container runtime, orchestration
layer, and network, including the ability to start, stop, reorder, replay, and
drop messages; (ii)~act as the \emph{operator} of the deployment, with the
motive to appear compliant---this is the distinguishing case for evidence
systems, where the party producing the record is the party under scrutiny;
(iii)~supply arbitrary untrusted content into the agent's context (indirect
prompt injection~\cite{greshake2023,injecagent}); and (iv)~compose otherwise
permitted skills and tool calls into sequences of its choosing.

We assume: \textbf{(A1)}~the signature scheme and hash function are
existentially unforgeable and collision-resistant; \textbf{(B1)}~the Attesting
Environment preserves confidentiality of its signing key and integrity of its
measured code---an assumption known to be imperfect in practice under
architectural and transient-execution
attacks~\cite{costan2016,foreshadow}; \textbf{(C1)}~the platform attestation
root (chip vendor or its delegate) does not issue forged endorsements---a
\emph{party} assumption, which is exactly why the standard caps vendor-rooted
evidence at Tier~2 absent independent anchoring; and \textbf{(D1)}~verifiers
can obtain published reference values and, for the properties below,
communicate with at least one other verifier or monitor.

\subsection{Security properties}
We state four properties an evidence system should satisfy, each relative to
the assumptions above.

\begin{description}[itemsep=2pt,leftmargin=1.4em]
\item[P1 (Verdict integrity).] For each token, the verdict was produced by
  the authorized policy bundle evaluating the committed snapshot. \emph{Holds
  under (A1),(B1),(C1)}: the enclave measurement pins the policy engine, the
  bundle hash pins the rules, and the signature binds the verdict to
  $H(\sigma_t^{\mathrm{snap}})$.
\item[P2 (Execution fidelity).] The evidence describes the action that
  actually executed. \emph{Does not hold from attestation alone}: it requires
  complete mediation of the effect channel (Section~\ref{sec:mediation}).
  Without one of the three mediation options, $\mathcal{A}$ mounts snapshot
  substitution and P2 fails while P1 still holds---an authenticated,
  false record.
\item[P3 (History integrity: append-only, no omission).] No prior step can be
  altered, reordered, or silently dropped from a chain a verifier observes.
  \emph{Holds within an observed prefix} under (A1) by hash chaining and
  monotonic indices; \emph{fails against truncation} at the head, since a
  verifier who has seen nothing later cannot distinguish ``no further steps''
  from ``steps withheld.'' The standard therefore requires anchoring within a
  bounded interval $\Delta$, which reduces the undetectable window to at most
  $\Delta$ and makes a missing anchor itself an alarm.
\item[P4 (Non-equivocation).] All relying parties see the same history.
  \emph{Does not hold from signing and anchoring alone}: $\mathcal{A}$ can
  fork the chain and present divergent prefixes to different verifiers
  (split-view), a known attack on transparency
  systems~\cite{rfc6962,ctgossip,chase2016}. Mitigation requires
  cross-verifier consistency checking (gossip), a witness quorum
  co-signing roots~\cite{chainiac}, or anchoring to a ledger with
  single-history consensus; the standard requires one of these for Tier-3
  claims where multiple relying parties act on the same agent's evidence.
\end{description}

\subsection{Formal treatment of P2 and P4}
\label{sec:proofs}

P1 and P3 follow directly from standard signature and hash-chain arguments.
P2 and P4 are the properties round-one analysis showed do \emph{not} follow
from attestation and chaining alone, so we state them precisely.

\begin{definition}[Evidenced execution]
\label{def:evidenced}
Fix an agent identity $\iota$ and a relying party $R$. An effect $e$ executed
at $R$ is \emph{evidenced} if there exists a token $\tau$ accepted by a
verifier such that $\tau$ commits to a snapshot digest
$H(\sigma^{\mathrm{snap}})$, a target resource $r$, and verdict
$d = \textsc{allow}$, where $\sigma^{\mathrm{snap}}$ describes exactly $e$ and
$r$ is the endpoint at which $e$ occurred.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Execution-fidelity experiment $\mathsf{Exp}^{\mathrm{fid}}$]
$\mathcal{A}$ controls the host, network, and operator role as in
Section~\ref{sec:security}. $\mathcal{A}$ interacts arbitrarily with the
Attesting Environment $\mathcal{E}$, obtaining tokens and capabilities of its
choice. $\mathcal{A}$ wins if a compliant relying party $R$ executes an
effect $e$ that is \emph{not} evidenced per Definition~\ref{def:evidenced}.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Execution fidelity under capability-bound dispatch]
\label{thm:fidelity}
Let $\mathcal{E}$ release, for each \textsc{allow} verdict, a single-use
capability $c = \mathrm{Sign}_{k_{\mathcal{E}}}(H(\sigma^{\mathrm{snap}})
\,\|\, r \,\|\, \mathrm{nonce})$, and let compliant $R$ execute a request
only if it presents a $c$ that (i)~verifies under an enclave key whose
measurement matches a published reference value, (ii)~names $R$'s own
resource $r$, (iii)~carries an unused nonce, and (iv)~matches the request's
canonical form against $H(\sigma^{\mathrm{snap}})$. Then for any PPT
$\mathcal{A}$,
\[
\Pr[\mathcal{A} \text{ wins } \mathsf{Exp}^{\mathrm{fid}}]
\;\le\; \mathbf{Adv}^{\mathrm{EUF\text{-}CMA}}_{\mathrm{Sig}}(\mathcal{A})
\;+\; \mathbf{Adv}^{\mathrm{coll}}_{H}(\mathcal{A}),
\]
under assumptions (A1), (B1), (C1).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Suppose $\mathcal{A}$ wins: $R$ executed $e$ with no token evidencing $e$ at
$r$. By $R$'s check~(i), the presented $c$ verified under $k_{\mathcal{E}}$,
which by (B1) never leaves $\mathcal{E}$ and by (C1) is bound to the attested
measurement; so either $\mathcal{A}$ forged a signature---bounding this case
by $\mathbf{Adv}^{\mathrm{EUF\text{-}CMA}}$---or $c$ was genuinely issued by
$\mathcal{E}$. In the latter case, by check~(iv) the executed request's
canonical form hashes to the $H(\sigma^{\mathrm{snap}})$ inside $c$. If
$\sigma^{\mathrm{snap}}$ did not describe $e$, then two distinct canonical
forms share a digest, bounding this case by
$\mathbf{Adv}^{\mathrm{coll}}_{H}$. Otherwise $\sigma^{\mathrm{snap}}$
describes $e$; by check~(ii) the resource matches, and $\mathcal{E}$ issues
$c$ only on an \textsc{allow} verdict whose token it also emits---so $e$ is
evidenced, contradicting the win condition. Check~(iii) prevents replay of a
capability against a second effect. \end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Necessity of mediation]
\label{prop:necessity}
Without a check binding the executed request to $H(\sigma^{\mathrm{snap}})$
at the point of effect, $\Pr[\mathcal{A} \text{ wins }
\mathsf{Exp}^{\mathrm{fid}}] = 1$ for an adversary controlling the host.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\mathcal{A}$ submits $\sigma$ describing benign $e'$, obtains an
\textsc{allow} token, and dispatches $e \ne e'$ using host-held credentials.
No verifier check is violated: the token is genuine and $\mathcal{E}$'s
statement (``policy allows $e'$'') is true. $e$ is unevidenced, so
$\mathcal{A}$ wins with certainty. \end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:necessity} is why C7.1.4 is a requirement rather than a
recommendation: an evidence architecture without effect mediation admits a
total break of P2 while every signature verifies.

\begin{definition}[Equivocation]
$\mathcal{A}$ equivocates if two verifiers $V_1, V_2$ accept chains
$\Lambda_1, \Lambda_2$ for the same $\iota$ such that neither is a prefix of
the other.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Equivocation is detectable and attributable]
\label{thm:equiv}
Let each chain root at index $i$ be signed by $\mathcal{E}$, and let $V_1$
and $V_2$ exchange (root, index) pairs---by direct gossip, via a witness
quorum of which at least one member is honest, or through a
single-history ledger. If $\mathcal{A}$ equivocates, then under (A1) and
(D1) the honest parties jointly hold two validly signed roots at a common
index with distinct values, which constitutes a non-repudiable proof of
equivocation attributable to $k_{\mathcal{E}}$; detection probability is $1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Non-prefix chains differ at some least index $i$. Both $V_1$ and $V_2$ hold
signed roots at $i$; exchanging them under (D1) reveals distinct values.
Forging either root reduces to signature forgery, excluded by (A1). The pair
is self-contained evidence, so detection does not depend on trusting either
verifier's account. \end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv} clarifies the requirement's shape: gossip does not
\emph{prevent} equivocation, it makes it detectable and attributable---the
same guarantee Certificate Transparency provides~\cite{rfc6962,chase2016},
and the reason C7.3.3 accepts any of three mechanisms that establish a shared
view. Without such a mechanism, the split-view attack succeeds silently.

\subsection{Constructing Tier 4}
\label{sec:tier4}
Tier~4 requires that verification gate operation: the system cannot perform
in-scope actions unless integrity holds. This is a construction, not an
assertion, and it follows directly from mediation option~2 of
Section~\ref{sec:mediation}. Let the relying party accept a request only if
it carries a capability $c_t$ committing to $H(\sigma_t^{\mathrm{snap}})$,
the target resource, and a fresh nonce, signed by the enclave key whose
measurement matches a published reference value. Then:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item if the enclave is not running, or its measurement does not match, no
  valid $c_t$ can be produced, and every in-scope request is refused at the
  relying party---the system \emph{fails closed by construction} rather than
  by operator policy;
\item the halting decision is therefore \emph{not under operator control},
  which is the property Tier~4 claims and which an in-process ``halt'' flag
  cannot provide, since a compromised host can patch it out;
\item the residual dependency is the relying party's enforcement and the
  attestation root (C1), both of which must appear in the trust-assumption
  disclosure.
\end{itemize}

This construction also clarifies the vendor-root tension: a TEE attestation
alone remains Tier~2, because (C1) is a party assumption. A deployment
reaches Tier~3 by composing it with independent anchoring so that a verifier
need not rely solely on the vendor's word about which code ran, and Tier~4 by
additionally making valid evidence a precondition of execution. The tiers
therefore grade \emph{trust structure}, not mechanism labels---consistent
with the standard's own rule that cryptography is a mechanism, not a tier.

\subsection{Deployability of capability-bound dispatch}
\label{sec:deploy}

Theorem~\ref{thm:fidelity} places a real burden on the ecosystem: relying
parties must verify capabilities. This is the strongest objection to the
architecture, and plausibly why it does not already exist. Three observations
bound the difficulty.

\emph{The mechanism is not new.} Action-bound capabilities are the classical
capability model~\cite{dennis1966,levy1984} in modern dress, and macaroons
already provide exactly the needed shape---bearer tokens with cryptographic
caveats, verifiable by the target service without contacting the
issuer~\cite{macaroons}, deployed over ordinary HTTP headers. A relying-party
check is a signature verification plus a caveat comparison, not new
infrastructure.

\emph{Adoption is incremental, and the first steps need no third party.}
Mediation options 1 and 3 (Section~\ref{sec:mediation}) are unilateral: an
operator can terminate egress inside the enclave, or route it through an
attested proxy, with no cooperation from anyone. Within an organization, the
API gateway is already a relying party under the same administrative control,
so capability checking can be enabled there first. Cross-organizational
enforcement follows contractually (as SOC~2 evidence requirements already
propagate through vendor agreements), and only then by standardization---%
which is the purpose of the EAT profile work in Appendix~\ref{sec:sdo}.

\emph{The residual gap is honest.} Endpoints that will never verify
capabilities---arbitrary third-party SaaS, legacy systems---can be covered
only by options 1 or 3, whose guarantee is weaker: they shrink the trusted
computing base rather than removing host involvement, and a deployment must
disclose which option covers which action class. We do not claim a universal
solution; we claim a specified property, three implementations of it with
different residual trust, and a disclosure requirement that makes the
difference legible to a relying party.

\section{Gap Analysis: What Existing Frameworks Already Require}
\label{sec:gap}

This section characterizes where \poc{} sits relative to existing frameworks.
We describe it as a \emph{gap analysis} rather than an evaluation: it
measures coverage of our own requirement set, so it cannot by construction
validate that set. Its purpose is descriptive---to locate the layer \poc{}
occupies---and its limitations are stated below.

\subsection{Method}
Following the coding methodology of HAARF~\cite{haarf2026}, every \poc{}
requirement was coded against eight frameworks as Exact Match (equivalent
clause in scope and intent), Partial Match (same topic, but without
operator-independent evidence or at lesser depth), or No Match. Coverage
$=(\mathrm{EM}+\mathrm{PM})/N$ over the $N=116$ requirements. Coding is at
section granularity with per-row rationales and clause attributions; the
sheet, rubric, and computation script are public, so any reader can re-derive
the numbers or re-code a framework.

\subsection{Results}
Figure~\ref{fig:coverage} and Table~\ref{tab:coverage} report the mapping.
Two patterns are consistent. First, the partial-match band is wide and the
exact-match band thin: NIST AI RMF attains the highest coverage with zero
exact matches, because governance frameworks require nearly every control
\poc{} verifies while never requiring mechanism-generated evidence that the
control held. Second, no-match concentrates in the evidence-property,
tier-grading, and disclosure chapters: no coded framework grades evidence by
how independently it can be verified, requires trust-assumption disclosure,
or specifies self-enforcing execution.

\subsection{Limitations of this analysis}
\label{sec:gaplimits}
Four limitations bound what may be concluded. \textbf{(i)~Direction.} The
metric asks how much of \poc{} each framework covers; a novel requirement set
scores incumbents low by construction, so these numbers argue that \poc{}
occupies a distinct layer---not that it is correct or necessary.
\textbf{(ii)~Single coder.} The seed coding was performed by one author, and
inter-rater reliability is therefore undefined. The public rubric specifies a
two-coder protocol with Cohen's $\kappa$~\cite{cohen1960} reported per
framework, and adversarial re-coding by parties who did not author the
requirements; that study is planned and its results will supersede
Table~\ref{tab:coverage}. \textbf{(iii)~Granularity.} Codings are assigned at
section level and inherited by the requirements within a section, which
overstates uniformity. \textbf{(iv)~One-directional.} We do not report the
reverse mapping---requirements those frameworks impose that \poc{} omits
(e.g., NIST AI RMF's organizational governance functions, ISO/IEC~42001's
management-system obligations, SOC~2's availability and processing-integrity
criteria). \poc{} is deliberately narrower than any of them; a complete
comparison must show both directions, and the reverse map is future work.

\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

We implemented the pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:arch} and evaluated it along
four axes: correctness against the requirements, resistance to the attacks of
Section~\ref{sec:security}, cost, and utility. The implementation is
open source in the specification repository.

\paragraph{What is implemented, and what is modelled.} Real: Ed25519 signing
and verification, SHA-256 hash chaining with monotonic indices, canonical
serialization, path-aware policy evaluation over a bounded summary,
capability minting and relying-party verification, anchoring, gossip-based
equivocation detection, and fail-closed behaviour on evidence-pipeline
failure. Modelled: the hardware TEE. No TDX/SEV-SNP machine was available, so
the Attesting Environment is an in-process object whose signing key is never
exposed through its API and whose measurement is a digest of the policy-engine
identity and bundle. This reproduces the protocol structure the theorems
concern, but not hardware isolation; \textbf{reported latencies therefore
exclude enclave transitions and are a lower bound}. Measurements: Apple M2
Max, Python 3.11, single core, 5{,}000 iterations after 200 warm-up steps.

\subsection{Utility cost of path-aware authorization}
\label{sec:utility}
The measurement that matters most for deployability is what path-awareness
costs in refused legitimate work. We ran 2{,}000 randomized workflows (70\%
benign, 30\% attempting read-then-egress escalation) through the path-aware
monitor.

\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}

\caption{Utility cost on 2{,}000 randomized workflows. Without a
declassification point, label creep refuses nearly half of benign work.}
\label{tab:utility}

The result reproduces, in the agent setting, the classical information-flow
finding that motivated Section~\ref{sec:ifc}: a monitor accumulating labels
along a path suffers \emph{label creep} and becomes unusable, and the
practical remedy is not a weaker policy but an explicit declassification
point---here a redaction step that resets the accumulated label before
egress. Adding one eliminates false rejections at a cost of 2.8 points of
detection. The design implication is a requirement-level one: \textbf{a
deployment that mandates path-aware authorization without specifying where
declassification occurs will refuse roughly half of its legitimate work},
and no amount of implementation tuning fixes that. We regard this as the
strongest argument in the paper for treating IFC as the intellectual parent of
path-aware authorization rather than as unrelated prior art.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
The TEE is modelled, so enclave transition cost is unmeasured and end-to-end
latency in a hardware deployment will be higher. The implementation is
single-process, excluding network and IPC costs between gateway, enclave, and
relying party. Workloads are synthetic: the qualitative utility result (label
creep, remedied by declassification) is robust, but the specific 42.2\% is a
property of our workload model, not a universal constant. The implementation
is Python, so absolute latencies are conservative and the durable result is
the relative component breakdown. The privacy-preserving evidence options of
the Privacy domain are specified but not implemented.

\subsection{Remaining design parameters}
Two parameters are set by policy rather than measurement.
The \textbf{anchoring interval $\Delta$} bounds the window in which head
truncation is undetectable (Section~\ref{sec:security});
Table~\ref{tab:delta} gives working guidance derived from the volume of
in-scope action a single window can conceal.
For the \textbf{privacy path}, an implementation admitting zero-knowledge
evidence must state the relation proven, the setup assumption, and the leakage
profile: ``uses ZKPs'' is not a conformance claim, and the tier assigned
depends on setup provenance, since a single-party trusted setup reintroduces
exactly the party-trust the threshold forbids.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Standards-integration targets (ISO/IEC JTC~1/SC~42, IETF RATS, ACS, NIST
AI~600-1) and the phased implementation roadmap are given in
Appendices~\ref{sec:sdo} and~\ref{sec:roadmapapp}; this section states what
the work does not yet establish.

\subsection{Limitations and status}
Six limitations are stated deliberately.
\textbf{(1)~The TEE is modelled, not deployed.} The reference implementation
exercises the full protocol and every check in it, but the attesting
environment is in-process: enclave transition cost is unmeasured, and a
hardware deployment will exceed the reported latencies. Deploying on TDX or
SEV-SNP and re-measuring is the immediate next step.
\textbf{(2)~Determinism boundary.} \poc{} verifies execution facts, not
output correctness, fairness, or intent; validation remains a human
responsibility, and an in-scope harmful action is a safety question the
standard explicitly does not claim to solve.
\textbf{(3)~Trust is reduced, not eliminated.} Tier~3--4 claims rest on
assumptions (A1),(B1),(C1) of Section~\ref{sec:security}; TEE compromise and
vendor-root failure are out of scope by assumption, and enclave attack
literature~\cite{costan2016,foreshadow} shows these assumptions are
imperfect. The standard's response is mandatory disclosure, not a claim of
trustlessness.
\textbf{(4)~Gap analysis is single-coder and one-directional}
(Section~\ref{sec:gaplimits}); the multi-coder study with $\kappa$ and the
reverse mapping are pending.
\textbf{(5)~Binary-threshold formalization.} The Tier-2/Tier-3 line is under
dedicated cryptographic and complexity-theoretic review, including
impossibility results bounding what agentic verification can achieve
efficiently; a formal characterization may move the line.
\textbf{(6)~Availability coupling.} Tier-4 fail-closed operation makes
availability depend on the evidence path; deployments must assess and
disclose this, and the construction of Section~\ref{sec:tier4} deliberately
places the halt outside operator control, which is a safety property and an
operational risk simultaneously.

Open working-group questions---identity/authorization ownership,
verifiable-but-unlinkable identity, evidence continuity across jurisdictions
as a possible fifth evidence property, and continuous-monitoring
cadence---are tracked publicly in the specification's open-issues register.

\subsection{Document status}
\poc{} is a working draft under public comment, developed openly with a
public specification repository, audit checklist, coding sheets, and figure
tooling. This paper documents the standard, its formal treatment, and a reference
implementation with an attack harness and measurements, all open source in the
specification repository. We invite adversarial review of the binary
threshold, the mediation requirement, the coverage coding, and---most
usefully---replication of the utility measurement on production agent traces,
which is the result most likely to change deployment guidance.

\section{Conclusion}

The claims-based world, in which relying parties accept an operator's word
for what an autonomous system did, does not scale to agents no one can
watch. \poc{} replaces attestation with mechanism-generated,
hardware-anchored, independently checkable evidence: six domains of
verifiable facts, a binary threshold that makes verifiability procurable,
an architecture that intercepts every consequential action, a reference
implementation in which eight attacks are refused or detected at a software
cost of \SI{191}{\micro\second} per step, and a coverage analysis showing
that no existing framework occupies this layer.
The specification, audit checklist, coding sheets, and reference tooling
are developed openly under the Advanced AI Society and are freely licensed
for implementation.

\section{Standards Integration}
\label{sec:sdo}
\emph{ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42:} \poc{} provides a technical realization for
active SC~42 workstreams~\cite{jtc1sc42,nemko2025,lexai2025}---a technical
annex for auditable runtime controls under ISO/IEC~42001 Clauses~8--9
(supplying cryptographically verifiable compliance evidence during
certification audits)~\cite{iso42001}, operationalization of ISO/IEC~23894
risk treatment as executable, hardware-verified policy
bundles~\cite{iso23894}, and an SC~42/SC~27 bridge coupling AI oversight
with confidential-computing standards~\cite{iec2025}.
\emph{IETF RATS:} an Internet-Draft establishing an AI-agent EAT
profile~\cite{rfc9711,tcg2024}---standardizing claims for lifecycle hook
types, policy-bundle hashes, Agent Bill of Materials (AgBOM) digests, and
step indices---and an extension of multi-verifier attestation to sub-agent
delegation chains~\cite{multiverifier}.
\emph{ACS:} \poc{} supplies the missing cryptographic layer for
ACS~\cite{acs2026,acsms2026}, anchoring its decision engine in TEEs and
signing decision tokens.
\emph{NIST AI 600-1:} \poc{} provides concrete implementations for the
generative-AI profile's measurement
controls~\cite{nist6001,ciphernorth2026,modulos2026}---enforcing that
deployment configurations cannot bypass security controls and satisfying
continuous security verification with attestation tokens for every tool
call and state transition.
